Ask a principal what they did the first time they met an AI chatbot, and many tell the same story. They typed in a question from their own board paper and watched the answer appear, neatly set out, in a few seconds. Then they tried a harder one. Then an essay prompt. Then a case study they were proud of. By the end of the evening, the question in their head was simple: can it do everything we ask our students to do?

For most of 2024 and 2025, that was the question in staff rooms across India. Can AI write the essay? Solve the problem set? Mark the paper? Schools tested it subject by subject, and the answer kept coming back yes, more often and more quickly than anyone expected.

That question has now run its course. In its place is a harder and more useful one.

Last year we asked what AI can do. This year the question is what only a person can do, and whether school is building it.The shift this series is built around.

Part one

Why "can it?" ran out.

"Can AI do this?" is a question about tasks. Tasks are exactly what technology improves at fastest. Every time a tool clears one, the line moves. A school that plans around that line has to replan every few months, and spends its energy reacting.

There is a quieter problem too. The more we ask what the tool can do, the more we trust it to do it. Researchers at Microsoft and Carnegie Mellon surveyed 319 people who use generative AI every week at work. The more confident people were in the AI, the less critical thinking they reported applying to its output. The more confident they were in themselves, the more they checked, questioned and improved it.

That finding matters for schools. If children grow up trusting the tool more than they trust their own thinking, they will check less, question less and learn less. The skill that protects them is not knowing more facts than the machine. It is having enough confidence in their own judgment to push back.

Part two

Two sides of the line.

The second question is steadier, because it asks what sits on the human side of the line and stays there. There is no fixed list, but some capacities hold up because they depend on being a person, in a place, with other people, taking responsibility for what happens next.

What AI now does wellWhat stays with people
Recalls facts and explains concepts→Deciding what is worth knowing, and why it matters to this child
Answers the question it is given→Asking the question nobody has asked yet
Produces a confident first draft→Judging whether it is right, and what is missing
Generates many options→Choosing one, and taking responsibility for it
Follows a known method→Applying it somewhere nobody prepared you for
Sounds caring→Noticing that someone is struggling, and staying with them

Read down the right-hand column and something becomes clear. None of it is new. Teachers have always said these are the things that matter most. What has changed is that the left-hand column used to be enough to do well at school. It no longer is.

There is an uncomfortable finding here as well. The OECD's first international survey of social and emotional skills found that 15-year-olds reported lower curiosity and creativity than 10-year-olds, across cities and backgrounds. The capacities on the human side of the line are the ones children arrive with in abundance. Too often, school wears them down.

Part three

What the lab builds on the human side.

At BE Human Labs we organise the human side of the line into four dimensions. Developed together, they move a person from Forward Thinker, someone who sees what is coming, to Active Shaper, someone who does something about it.

Awareness

Seeing clearly.

Knowing what you understand and what you don't. Noticing what is changing around you, and in you.

Capability

Judging and creating.

Weighing a plausible answer against a right one. Using what you know in a situation nobody prepared you for.

Connection

Working with people.

Trust, disagreement, persuasion and repair. Drawing on others to do what no one could alone.

Contribution

Acting on it.

Turning a good idea into something that actually happened, and owning the result.

Schools have long been strong in one part of capability: knowledge and execution. That is precisely the part machines now share. The other three dimensions, and the judgment end of capability, were treated as extras. They are now the main event.

Part four

What changes in a school.

None of this means throwing out the syllabus. Knowledge still matters, because you cannot judge an answer in a subject you know nothing about. What changes is how the day is spent and what is rewarded.

  1. Lessons are designed for thinking, not only for coverage.The same chapter, taught so that students do the reasoning. The test for every lesson: who did the thinking in this room?
  2. Assessment looks at reasoning, not only at answers.If a tool can produce the answer, the answer alone tells you little about the child. Ask them to explain, defend and apply it.
  3. More work is done together.Connection is a capacity, and it grows only through practice with other people: pairs, groups, mixed ages and real audiences.
  4. Students act on something real.At least once a term, a problem with a real audience and a real consequence, so contribution is practised, not just discussed.

Does this make students better at something AI already does well, or better at what only they can do?A test for any new programme, tool or timetable change. Both have a place. If every answer is the first, the school is preparing children for a test that is no longer being set.
