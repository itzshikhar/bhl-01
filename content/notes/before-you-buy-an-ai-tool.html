The demo is always impressive. A sales team opens a laptop in the principal's office and shows an AI tutor answering a student's question in perfect Hindi, a dashboard glowing with progress charts, a lesson plan generated in seconds. The price seems reasonable for what it does. The contract is for three years. The question nobody in the room asks is the one that matters: will our children learn more?

School leaders are being offered AI tools for tutoring, marking, lesson planning, attendance and parent communication. Some will help. Many will not. Most are sold with a demo that tells you little about what happens in a real classroom in month three.

UNESCO's global review of technology in education found that much of the evidence for edtech comes from the companies selling it. Its message to school systems was clear: judge technology by what it does for learning, not by what it promises.

What has changed in India

The Digital Personal Data Protection Rules were notified in November 2025. Anyone processing the personal data of under-18s will need verifiable parental consent, and behavioural tracking and targeted advertising aimed at children are prohibited. Most obligations take effect within 18 months. Every tool your school uses with children now needs a clear answer on consent and data.

Part one

The ten questions.

Ask them of the vendor, and then of yourselves. They fall into five groups: learning, safety, fit, money and proof.

#QuestionAsk the vendorRed flag
1What learning problem does this solve?
Learning		"Name the problem in one sentence, and how you measure it."The answer is about features, not learning.
2Who does the thinking?
Learning		"Does it give students hints or answers?"It hands over full solutions on request.
3What is the evidence, beyond the demo?
Learning		"Show me results from schools like ours, and let me call one."Only testimonials, or studies they paid for and won't share.
4What happens to our children's data?
Safety		"What do you collect, where is it stored, who sees it, how is it deleted, and how will you meet DPDP consent rules?"Vague answers, overseas storage without safeguards, or data used to train their models.
5Is it safe and suitable for this age?
Safety		"What stops a child seeing harmful content or sharing personal details?"No age settings, no content filters, no reporting route.
6Does it work for our students?
Fit		"In our languages, on our devices, with our internet?"Needs new devices or constant high-speed connectivity.
7Does it save teacher time or add to it?
Fit		"How many hours of training and setup, and who checks the output?"Teachers become data-entry staff for the tool.
8What does it really cost over three years?
Money		"Licences, devices, training, support and price rises, in writing."Low first-year price with no cap on later increases.
9Can we leave?
Money		"If it doesn't work, can we take our data and stop paying?"Long lock-ins or data you cannot export.
10How will we know in six months?
Proof		"What will you commit to, and how will we measure it together?"No measure agreed before signing.

Part two

How to score it.

Mark each question as clear, unclear or worrying.

Clear on all ten

Pilot it.

Start with one grade or department for one term, with question 10's measure agreed in advance.

Several unclear

Go smaller.

A shorter, free or low-cost trial before any contract. Ask for answers in writing.

Any worrying on 2, 4 or 5

Wait.

One worrying answer on thinking, data or safety is reason enough to walk away for now.

Part three

A pilot before a contract.

  1. Before

    Agree the measure.

    Decide what success looks like and how you will check it: a test, a teacher survey, time saved. Record where students are today.

  2. Weeks 1 to 4

    Train, then use fully.

    Train the pilot teachers properly and use the tool as intended. A half-used pilot proves nothing.

  3. Weeks 5 to 10

    Listen.

    Ask teachers and students what is working. Watch for the time it takes, not just the results it shows.

  4. End of term

    Decide on evidence.

    Compare against your baseline and a similar class that did not use the tool. A vendor confident in their product will welcome this.
