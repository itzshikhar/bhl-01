In a recent experiment, nearly a thousand high school students were split into three groups to practise mathematics. One group had an ordinary AI assistant. One had an AI tutor designed with teachers to give hints, not answers. One had no AI at all. During practice, the AI groups did far better. Then the researchers took the AI away and gave everyone an exam.

The students who had used the ordinary assistant scored 17% lower than students who never had AI at all. They had solved more problems and learned less. The tutor group, whose AI made them do the work, did no worse than the control group.

Getting the answer and learning the answer have come apart.The single most important finding for any school deciding how students should use AI.

Part one

The crutch effect.

The pattern shows up again and again. In an MIT Media Lab study, people writing essays with ChatGPT showed the lowest brain connectivity of any group, and later struggled to recall what they had written. Researchers at Microsoft and Carnegie Mellon found that the more people trusted AI, the less critical thinking they applied to its output.

These are early studies, and the MIT work is small and not yet peer reviewed. But they point the same way as decades of learning research: we learn what we do, not what we watch. If the tool does the struggle, the tool gets the learning.

Indian schools cannot afford this effect. PARAKH Rashtriya Sarvekshan 2024 tested over 21 lakh students. Class 9 students averaged 37% in mathematics, and only 28% could apply percentages. Those are the students now receiving free AI on their phones.

Part two

Who does the thinking?

Schools often treat critical thinking as something to add: a new period, a new workbook, a new elective. Thinking does not grow that way. It grows like fitness, through small, repeated effort built into the normal day. The question for every lesson is who is doing the thinking.

Teacher thinks

The teacher explains and solves. Students watch. Clear, but students exercise very little.

→
Tool thinks

AI explains and solves. Students copy. Fast, and the learning goes to the tool.

→
Student thinks

Students try first, explain, check and fix. Slower, and the learning stays with them.

The guided tutor in the study worked because it was designed to keep students in the third column. Teachers can do the same with routines, without any technology at all.

Part three

Five routines.

Each takes two to five minutes. None needs new material. Use one in every lesson for a week, then add the next.

  1. Think first.Before any help, from a teacher, peer or AI, students spend two minutes on their own attempt and write it down. A wrong first attempt is fine. The point is that their mind went first.
  2. Explain it to a partner.Students explain their answer to a peer in under a minute. The partner asks one "why". Explaining shows a student what they understand and what they only recognise.
  3. Predict before you check.Before an experiment, a calculation or a reading, students commit to a prediction. The gap between prediction and result is where learning happens.
  4. Fix the wrong answer.Put a plausible wrong answer on the board, ideally one an AI tool produced. Students find the error and explain it. This builds judgment, and teaches them that confident answers can be wrong.
  5. Leave with a question.At the end of the lesson, each student writes one question they still have. Read them. They tell you more about the lesson than any test.

Part four

Making it stick.

A routine only works when it stops being an event. Students stop waiting for permission to think, because thinking becomes how the class works. Schools that already use peer learning will find routines two and four fit easily. The order below builds each habit on the one before it.

  1. Week 1

    One routine, every lesson.

    Pick "Think first". Use it at the start of every lesson for five days. On Friday, ask students what changed.

  2. Weeks 2 to 3

    Add a partner.

    Layer in "Explain it to a partner". Students now think, then talk. Listen in and note the misunderstandings you hear.

  3. Weeks 4 to 6

    Bring in the wrong answer.

    Once a week, use an AI-generated answer with a flaw. Students learn to read AI output critically, which is the skill the crutch effect erodes.

  4. Term end

    Compare.

    Look at one unit test against last year's. Ask students which routine helped most. Share both with colleagues.
