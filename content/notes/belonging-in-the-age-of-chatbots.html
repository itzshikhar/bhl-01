Imagine a Class 10 student, two weeks before her pre-boards, awake at one in the morning. She is anxious, and she does not want to wake her parents or message a friend who might tell others. So she opens an AI app and types out how she feels. It answers kindly, instantly, and without judgment. It never gets tired of her. It never tells anyone.

It is easy to see the appeal. It is also worth taking seriously, because this is no longer a rare scene.

Part one

What a chatbot offers, and what it can't.

For some young people, a chatbot is a first step: a way to put a feeling into words before saying it to a person. For others, it may become a substitute for the harder and more rewarding work of human connection.

An AI companion offersA person can offer
Available at any hour→Notices changes over weeks, and checks in unasked
Never judges, always agrees→Disagrees kindly, and helps you see another side
Keeps every secret→Knows when a secret is too dangerous to keep, and acts
Sounds caring→Is responsible for your wellbeing
Asks nothing of you→Lets you care for them too, which is how belonging grows

Connection is built through friction: being misunderstood, making up, being known.A tool that always agrees offers none of that.

Part two

What the evidence says, and doesn't.

The same survey offers some reassurance. Two in three teenagers found AI conversations less satisfying than talking to people, and four in five still spent more time with real friends. But a quarter had shared personal information, such as their name or location, with an AI companion. Younger teens were more likely to trust its advice.

We should be careful. These figures come from one survey of 1,060 teenagers outside India. We do not yet know how common these patterns are among Indian students, or how they differ between cities and towns, or between boys and girls. Anyone who gives a confident number for India should be asked where it came from. What we do know is that Indian teenagers now have free access to capable AI on their phones, and that loneliness is highest at exactly their age.

Part three

Connection by design.

Schools cannot police what students do at one in the morning. They can make sure that, when a student needs someone, a person is the easier choice. At BE Human Labs, connection is one of the four dimensions we build, and it grows in layers.

Layer one

Every child known.

Small, stable groups with one caring adult, such as a mentor or advisory group that meets weekly. Every student should be able to name an adult who knows them.

Layer two

Peers who listen.

Trained older students and buddy systems make talking to a person the first step, not the last.

Layer three

Open talk about AI.

Help students understand what AI is and is not: it does not know them and cannot be responsible for them. Do this without shaming those who use it.

Layer four

Help that is easy to reach.

Every student knows who to go to, and that going is normal. Every teacher knows what to do next.

If a student is struggling

Any sign that a student is in distress should go to a school counsellor or a trusted adult straight away, whatever tool they have been talking to. Make sure every teacher knows that path. In India, Tele-MANAS offers free mental health support by phone on 14416.
