The meeting usually goes well until someone asks the question. The principal has explained the new approach: more student thinking, more explaining, more projects. Heads nod. Then a senior teacher, the one whose Class 12 results the school advertises every May, raises a hand. "If we try this, what happens to our board results?"

It is the right question. Results matter to parents, to students' futures and to a school's standing. A leader who ignores them will not lead for long. But the question assumes a trade-off that, for the most part, is not there.

Part one

The papers have already moved.

Board papers are changing under our feet. CBSE raised competency-based questions in Class 12 from 40% to 50% in 2024-25, matching Class 10. These questions present unfamiliar situations and ask students to apply what they know. A student who memorised the textbook answer struggles. A student who understood can reason through.

The risk to results is no longer in changing how we teach. It is in not changing.Rote preparation is aimed at a paper that is disappearing.

Part two

Where results and thinking overlap.

In 2013, a team of learning scientists led by John Dunlosky reviewed hundreds of studies on ten study techniques students commonly use. Only two were rated high utility: testing yourself, and spreading practice over time. The techniques students use most, rereading and highlighting, were rated low.

TechniqueUtilityWhat it looks like in a thinking classroom
Practice testingHighStudents quiz themselves and each other before checking notes.
Distributed practiceHighShort revisits of old topics every week, not one cram before exams.
Self-explanationModerateExplaining each step to a partner in your own words.
Elaborative questioningModerateAsking "why is this true?" about every fact.
Interleaved practiceModerateMixing problem types so students choose the method.
Rereading, highlighting, summarisingLowWhat most students do the night before, and why it feels productive but isn't.

Look at the right-hand column. The techniques that work best are the habits of a thinking classroom: testing, explaining, questioning, choosing. They are also the habits of a Forward Thinker. Good results and future-ready students come from the same daily practice.

What boards reward

Application.

Using concepts in unfamiliar, real-life situations.

The overlap

Thinking habits.

Self-testing, explaining, questioning and choosing the method.

What the future needs

Judgment.

Analytical thinking, transfer and confidence in your own reasoning.

Part three

Where the real risk is.

The risk is not in thinking-based teaching. It is in changing everything at once, in the wrong year, without measuring. Four rules keep a school safe.

  1. Start in the middle years.Build habits in Classes 6 to 8, where there is room to adjust, before asking exam years to change.
  2. Change the practice, not the syllabus.Teach the same content in a way that makes students test, explain and apply it.
  3. Protect revision in exam years.Students still need focused practice on the paper they will sit. Use self-testing, not rereading.
  4. Measure both.Track results and thinking side by side, so the decision rests on evidence rather than hope.

Part four

Two scoreboards.

Schools tend to keep one scoreboard: marks. Keep two. The first is the one you already have. The second tracks the four dimensions that turn a good student into an Active Shaper. Report both to staff each term, and in time to parents.

Scoreboard one

Results.

Unit tests, pre-boards and board results, as now. Add one unfamiliar problem to every unit test.

Scoreboard two

Thinking.

Awareness, capability, connection and contribution, using short rubrics teachers fill in monthly.

What to tell parents
