For most of history, learning was limited by what a student could get. A book, a teacher, someone who could explain the idea, a place to practise, an expert to check the work, a mentor to say what came next. Each of these was scarce, and institutions were built to ration them.

AI has made almost all of that abundant. Information, explanation, practice cases, feedback and even a suggested next step can now be produced on demand, for anyone, at any hour.

That does not make learning easy. It moves the hard part. What stays scarce is what only the student can supply: the effort to work through something, the awareness of what they do not yet understand, and the persistence to keep going when nothing outside them is pushing.

External barriers ask whether something can be supplied to you. Internal barriers ask whether you can use it once you have it.The dividing line this note uses throughout.

Part one

Five barriers that have fallen.

Each of these used to decide who learned well. Each is now cheap. The teacher does not disappear from any of them, but where the teacher adds value moves.

Access

Can I get the information I need?

Used to depend on
Having a source: a teacher, a book, an institution.
Where the teacher moves
Curation: finding the right thing and knowing what to trust.

Understanding

Can I make sense of it and see why it matters?

Used to depend on
Finding someone who could explain it well.
Where the teacher moves
Meaning: connecting the idea to why it matters for this student.

Practice and variety

Do I get to do it, across real cases?

Used to depend on
Being placed where real exposure existed.
Where the teacher moves
Designing the struggle: choosing practice that stretches this student.

Feedback and guidance

Do I know what is right and how to improve?

Used to depend on
An expert's time and attention, always rationed.
Where the teacher moves
Feedback a student will act on, inside a relationship they trust.

Direction

Do I know what to do next?

Used to depend on
A mentor's experience mapping the next step.
Where the teacher moves
Deciding the destination. What is worth pursuing is still a values call.

Time cuts across all five. Each one used to cost weeks of waiting; now it costs seconds.

Part two

Five barriers that now decide.

When everything outside the student is available, the difference between two students sits inside them. These were always part of learning. They used to be propped up by scarcity, deadlines and a teacher in the room. Now they carry the weight on their own.

Effort

Am I working through it?

What changed
Scarcity used to force the struggle. Answers now arrive with none.
What sets students apart
Choosing effort. Students who engage deeply pull ahead.

Recall

Can I bring it back when I need it?

What changed
Facts can be retrieved instantly.
What sets students apart
Fluency: ideas held well enough to think and decide fast.

Self-awareness

Do I know what I understand and what I do not?

What changed
A fixed path once did this for you. Now every path is open at once.
What sets students apart
Knowing what you do not know, and using it as a compass.

Persistence

Will I keep going when it is hard?

What changed
Deadlines and a classroom used to push. Flexibility removes the push.
What sets students apart
Keeping going with nothing outside forcing it.

Transfer

Can I use what I know somewhere new?

What changed
Standard execution, once valuable, is what AI now does well.
What sets students apart
Applying knowledge in a situation nobody prepared them for.

Which of these five hold your students back most? We can help you find out, with classroom visits and short conversations with faculty and students.

Part three

Pace and depth.

If the internal barriers decide, an institution needs a model that trains them on purpose. We use two tracks. Pace is how fast a student moves through one full loop on a topic and on to the next, across breadth. Depth picks up once a topic is chosen for real expertise. They meet at one stage: Application.

Pace of learning
Depth of skill
1
RecognitionDo I know this exists?Meeting the idea, its terms and examples.
2
Mental modelDo I understand how it works?Explaining it in your own words: cause, effect, what can be controlled.
3
RetrievalCan I bring it to mind when I need it?Pulling the right idea up when a problem appears.
4
ApplicationCan I actually use it, hands on?Doing the work, making mistakes, taking feedback, correcting.The hinge: end of pace, start of depth
5
Novel problemsCan I solve something I was not taught?Diagnosing a new problem, forming hypotheses, experimenting.
6
PatternsDo I see what keeps recurring?After many attempts, spotting the structures and failure modes.
7
GapsWhere does the current approach fall short?Naming the bottleneck that makes it still hard.
8
InnovationCan I create a better way?A new method or combination that removes the bottleneck.

Read the bars on the left. Pace runs from the first stage to Application, and a student repeats it topic after topic. Depth starts at Application and runs to Innovation, on the few topics a student chooses to master. Retrieval is recalling the tool; Application is using it. A novel problem is diagnosed within one case; a gap is diagnosed across many.

The target is a deep generalist: fast enough to cover breadth, deep enough on the right topics to actually innovate.Either one alone is not enough.

The quickest way to test this is on one real course. In a half day workshop, your faculty map a course they teach against the eight stages and see where it stops.

Part four

Who carries each stage, and how the lab helps.

The stages of learning do not change. What changes is who carries each one. The product leads where the work is organisational. The teacher leads where judgment cannot be automated. The student leads where only they can supply the effort. As you move down the stages, the lead moves from product, to teacher, to student.

Product

Carries what became abundant: sources, records, feedback, memory.

→
Teacher

Carries judgment: designing the struggle, setting real problems.

→
Student

Carries what only they can supply: effort, naming what is stuck, the idea.

That hand off does not happen by itself. Teachers need new skills before they can let a product carry the early stages. Students need structures that make effort and persistence normal. The institution needs assets that outlast any one cohort. This is the work the lab does with an institution, through four offerings.

People

Guide the change

We work alongside leadership and faculty as they redesign how teaching works. That means coaching teachers to check understanding when the submitted work looks right because AI produced it, and to mark the student's reasoning, not the artefact.

The institution gets teachers who are confident leading the middle stages.

Products

Scale it

We help shape the one platform where work happens: curated sources the institution stands behind, a record where no work gets lost, and a view of where students stall across a whole cohort.

The institution gets a product that carries the early stages, and sight it never had before.

Programs

Structure it

We design and run the first programs, then hand them over. Induction and faculty development come first, for teachers. Live projects, project reviews, an open problems seminar and incubation follow, for students.

The institution gets programs it can run on its own, with names its faculty already know.

Systems

Sustain it

We help build what no single teacher can: a bank of real problems from employers and labs, a practice bank, a repository of past projects, shared standards, and incentives that reward the new work.

The institution gets assets that become more valuable every year they are used.

Part five

A roadmap for institution leaders.

Every institution starts from a different place, so the pace will differ. The order matters more than the speed. Each phase asks one clear decision of leadership and leaves something visible behind, so you can judge progress before committing to the next.

  1. Phase 1

    Agree what success looks like.

    We spend time with your leadership, faculty and students to understand how learning, feedback and assessment work today. Together we agree the few outcomes that will count, and record where your students stand on them now.

    Your decision
    Name a senior sponsor, and choose the departments or campuses to start with.
    What you will have
    A clear baseline, and a shared definition of success your board can back.

    Most institutions start here. Phase 1 begins with one conversation with your leadership team.

  2. Phase 2

    Equip your first group of faculty.

    Your teachers learn to check real understanding when the work looks right because AI produced it, and to redesign assignments so students defend their choices. Teachers come first because everything after depends on them.

    Your decision
    Protect faculty time for development, and recognise the teachers who lead.
    What you will have
    A core group of faculty using redesigned assignments in their own classes.
  3. Phase 3

    Prove it in a pilot.

    The new way of working runs in your chosen departments, on one platform used by nearly every student and teacher there. The platform carries the routine stages, so teachers can spend their time where judgment matters.

    Your decision
    Commit to full use within the pilot, so the evidence is real.
    What you will have
    Students moving through topics faster, teachers spotting who is struggling earlier, and a fair comparison with the rest of the institution.
  4. Phase 4

    Connect students to real problems.

    Employers, research labs and the city bring live problems to campus. Students work on them under trained faculty, review past projects, and test their thinking with practitioners in the room.

    Your decision
    Open doors to industry and community partners, and make this work count toward assessment.
    What you will have
    Students solving problems nobody taught them, and employers who start to recognise your graduates.
  5. Phase 5

    Make it your institution's own.

    The model extends across the institution. Incubation and seed funding back the ideas worth building. Standards and incentives keep the new work rewarded, and every program moves fully into your team's hands.

    Your decision
    Embed the model in policy, budget and promotion criteria.
    What you will have
    An institution that runs the model on its own, with assets that grow more valuable every year, and graduates who stand out.
