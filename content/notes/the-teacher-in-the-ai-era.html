A government school teacher in India spends less than a fifth of her working hours actually teaching. The rest goes to records, surveys, mid-day meal registers, election duty and the hundred small tasks that surround a school. From 2026-27, she will also be asked to teach AI and computational thinking from Class 3, often before anyone has shown her how to use AI well herself.

This is the real starting point for any conversation about teachers and AI. It is not a story about machines replacing teachers. It is a story about teachers who have never had enough time for the part of the job that matters most, now facing a technology that could either give that time back or take even more of it.

Part one

Where a teacher's authority comes from.

For generations, part of a teacher's authority came from knowing things students did not. That source is thinner now. A student can ask a tool and get an answer faster than raising a hand. This can feel like a loss. It is better understood as a release, because the part of teaching that machines share was never the most important part.

The teacher's role beforeThe teacher's role now
Source of answers→Designer of questions and struggle
Explains to the whole class→Listens to how each student reasons
Covers the textbook→Chooses what is accurate, suitable and worth the time
Marks the answer→Assesses the reasoning behind it
Knows the subject→Knows the subject, the child, and how to learn with AI

Part two

Five roles that grow.

Question-designer

What will make them think?

Looks like
Writing the question that starts the lesson, and the problem students have to work out together.
Builds in students
Capability

Coach of thinking

Where did their reasoning go wrong?

Looks like
Listening to pairs explain, spotting the misunderstanding, asking the question that helps them fix it.
Builds in students
Awareness

Editor of resources

What is worth their time?

Looks like
Choosing from endless material, checking AI output for errors, adapting it to Indian context.
Builds in students
Judgment about sources

Anchor of relationships

Who needs what today?

Looks like
Knowing who is struggling at home, who needs a push, who needs patience. No tool knows this.
Builds in students
Connection

Model learner

How does an adult learn?

Looks like
Using AI in front of students, checking it, disagreeing with it, and saying "I don't know yet, let's find out."
Builds in students
Contribution and confidence

Children learn from people they trust. That relationship has always been the real medium of teaching.It matters more now, because it is the one thing in the room that cannot be replaced.

Part three

What teachers need from schools.

None of these roles can be adopted by memo. Teachers need four things, and each maps to one of the ways BE Human Labs works with schools.

Time

Systems sustain it

Designing good questions takes longer than delivering content. Something else has to give: fewer registers, shared planning, protected periods.

The school decides what to stop, so there is room to start.

Permission

People guide it

To try, to get it wrong, and to cover the syllabus differently. Teachers need to hear it from their principal, not just a circular.

The school decides that a failed experiment is not a failed teacher.

Practice

Programs structure it

Teachers learn new methods best by experiencing them as learners first, then trying them with support, then reflecting.

The school decides to invest in development, not one-off training days.

Tools

Products scale it

AI that takes the routine work, such as worksheets, translations and first-draft feedback, so time goes to students.

The school decides which tools are safe, and trains everyone to use them.

Part four

A development path for teachers.

The order matters. Each step depends on the one before it.

  1. Step 1

    Experience it as a learner.

    Teachers sit as students in a lesson built around thinking routines and AI used well. They feel what their students will feel.

  2. Step 2

    Try it in one class.

    Each teacher picks one chapter and teaches it the new way, with a colleague observing.

  3. Step 3

    Reflect with peers.

    Small groups of four to six teachers compare what changed in participation, independence and learning.

  4. Step 4

    Lead others.

    Teachers who have done it well run the next round for their colleagues. The school builds its own capacity.
